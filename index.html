The basics

What are peptides?

Peptides are short chains of amino acids, the same building blocks that make up proteins. By convention, a chain of roughly 2 to 50 amino acids is a peptide; anything longer is usually called a protein.

Your body already makes thousands of them. Many act as signaling molecules, telling cells and tissues what to do. Insulin, oxytocin and glucagon are all well-known examples.

Because they are so specific, peptides are a major focus of biochemical and pharmaceutical research. Scientists study synthetic versions in the lab to understand pathways involved in metabolism, growth signaling, tissue repair and cellular aging.

Research peptides are typically supplied lyophilized (freeze-dried powder) in sealed vials, which keeps them stable until they are reconstituted for laboratory work.

What they're known for

Common research areas

These are the categories you'll see across Origin Labs' catalog and in published literature. Descriptions reflect areas of scientific interest, not treatment claims.

Metabolic

Metabolic research

Studies of incretin and related pathways involved in glucose handling, appetite signaling and energy balance. Approved medications in this class exist and are available only through licensed providers.

GH

GH research

Peptides such as tesamorelin are studied for how they interact with growth hormone-releasing pathways. Tesamorelin itself is an FDA-approved drug for a specific medical use.

Regenerative

Regenerative research

Lab models of tissue repair, recovery and inflammation signaling. One of the most actively discussed areas in the peptide research community.

Dermal

Dermal research

Focused on skin biology: collagen, wound closure and cellular renewal in lab settings. Copper peptides such as GHK-Cu are a well-known example.
